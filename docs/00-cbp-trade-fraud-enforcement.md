# CBP and DOJ Are Coming After Tariff Errors: How to Protect Your Business

## What is the DOJ Trade Fraud Task Force doing about customs fraud?

The DOJ Trade Fraud Task Force — launched in August 2025 with 40 dedicated attorneys — has already recovered over [$140 million in trade fraud settlements](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/cody-herche-new-head-of-doj-trade-fraud-task-force-signals-aggressive-enforcement-shift) and is pursuing criminal prosecutions against importers who misclassify goods, misrepresent country of origin, or evade tariffs through transshipment schemes.

## How can importers protect themselves from CBP enforcement actions?

Importers can reduce enforcement risk by standardizing HTS classifications across all brokers, documenting country of origin determinations with supporting evidence, auditing valuation methodologies, and considering voluntary self-disclosure under DOJ's Corporate Enforcement Policy when compliance gaps are discovered.

If your import data has gaps, your classifications are inconsistent, or your documentation cannot survive a formal audit, those problems are about to become expensive.

## What Is the Trade Fraud Task Force and Why Should Importers Care?

In August 2025, DOJ [launched the Trade Fraud Task Force](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/departments-justice-and-homeland-security-partnering-cross-agency-trade-fraud-task-force) in partnership with CBP and Homeland Security Investigations. The task force is staffed with 40 attorneys and coordinates civil and criminal enforcement against importers who evade tariffs, misclassify goods, misrepresent country of origin, or smuggle prohibited products.

On February 23, 2026, Task Force Head Cody Matthew Herche delivered the keynote at the International Trade Investigations, Enforcement & Litigation conference in Arlington, Virginia. His message was unambiguous:

> "We are making a fundamental shift in the government's approach to trade fraud enforcement... advanced data analytics now enable investigators to see through shell games that previously obscured fraudulent conduct."
>
> — Cody Matthew Herche, Head of DOJ Trade Fraud Task Force, [February 2026 keynote](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/cody-herche-new-head-of-doj-trade-fraud-task-force-signals-aggressive-enforcement-shift)

This is not a task force in name only. Herche reported $140 million in recoveries to date, with "substantially more in the pipeline." The Chicago U.S. Attorney's Office has been [named lead partner](https://news.wttw.com/2026/02/24/chicago-s-federal-prosecutor-s-office-named-lead-partner-new-trade-fraud-task-force) in the task force, signaling dedicated prosecutorial resources.

## How Did False Claims Act Customs Recoveries Hit a Record?

Total recoveries under the [False Claims Act reached $6.8 billion](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/false-claims-act-settlements-and-judgments-exceed-68b-fiscal-year-2025) in fiscal year 2025 — the highest single-year total in the statute's history. A record number of those cases involved customs fraud, and [whistleblowers filed 1,297 qui tam lawsuits](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/false-claims-act-settlements-and-judgments-exceed-68b-fiscal-year-2025), also the highest ever recorded.

The False Claims Act allows the government to pursue civil penalties against anyone who makes a false statement material to a payment or obligation to the federal government. In the trade context, that includes filing inaccurate entry summaries, underreporting duties owed, or misrepresenting classification, valuation, or country of origin on customs declarations. Penalties under the FCA include treble damages — three times the amount of unpaid duties — plus per-claim penalties.

**Case in point:** Ceratizit USA LLC, a Charlotte-based distributor of tungsten carbide products, agreed to pay [$54.4 million to settle FCA allegations](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/ceratizit-usa-llc-agrees-pay-544m-settle-false-claims-act-allegations-relating-evaded-0) that it knowingly misrepresented country of origin on Chinese-manufactured products transshipped through Taiwan to evade Section 301 tariffs, and simultaneously misclassified the products under incorrect HTS codes. The whistleblower in the case received approximately $9.75 million.

## How Is CBP Using AI and Data Analytics for Enforcement?

CBP is no longer relying solely on random audits and manual reviews. In October 2025, the agency awarded a multi-million dollar contract to [Exiger for AI-powered detection of illicit transshipment](https://www.exiger.com/perspectives/exiger-wins-cbp-contract-detection-of-illicit-transshipment/) across global supply chains.

Exiger's platform gives CBP enforcement offices access to AI tools that can cross-reference import data and detect anomalies including:

- The same product classified under different HTS codes by different brokers
- Valuation shifts inconsistent with market conditions
- Origin declarations inconsistent with known supply chain logistics
- Importers claiming multiple tariff exemptions on the same goods
- Tariff classification, valuation, and country of origin inconsistencies with First Sale and USMCA claims

GingerControl is a trade compliance AI platform that helps importers, exporters, and customs brokers classify products, simulate tariff costs, and track policy changes. The same AI-powered data analysis that CBP uses to detect inconsistencies is the kind of analysis importers should be doing proactively — validating their own classifications before CBP flags them.

## What Are the Penalty Tiers for Customs Violations Under Section 1592?

[19 U.S.C. § 1592](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1592) establishes three tiers of penalties for customs violations — negligence, gross negligence, and fraud — with escalating consequences at each level.

| Culpability Level | Definition | Maximum Penalty | Example |
|-------------------|------------|-----------------|---------|
| **Negligence** | Failure to exercise reasonable care | 2x the unpaid duties, or 20% of dutiable value | Using an outdated HTS code without checking for updates |
| **Gross Negligence** | Actual knowledge or wanton disregard | 4x the unpaid duties, or 40% of dutiable value | Ignoring broker advice that a classification is incorrect |
| **Fraud** | Intentional false statement or omission | Domestic value of the merchandise | Deliberately misrepresenting country of origin |

Even unintentional misclassification — where a company simply did not exercise "reasonable care" in determining the correct code — can result in [Section 1592 penalties](https://www.cbp.gov/trade/programs-administration/penalties). The standard is not whether you intended to cheat. The standard is whether you took adequate steps to get it right.

For importers with high volumes and systematic classification errors, the exposure compounds rapidly. A single misclassification replicated across thousands of entries can produce seven-figure penalties. A country of origin misrepresentation can result in criminal prosecution of individual employees.

## What Exactly Is CBP and DOJ Targeting?

Based on recent enforcement actions and public statements from the Task Force, these are the specific areas drawing the most scrutiny:

**Country of origin misrepresentation.** The largest settlements — including the [$54.4 million Ceratizit case](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/ceratizit-usa-llc-agrees-pay-544m-settle-false-claims-act-allegations-relating-evaded-0) — involved companies declaring goods as originating from one country when they were actually manufactured in China and transshipped through a third country to evade Section 301 duties. Task Force Head Herche confirmed that [manipulation of country of origin is the most common scheme](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/doj-trade-fraud-task-force-head-6583390/) being prosecuted.

**HTS misclassification.** Both intentional and negligent misclassification are being pursued. DOJ resolved a criminal trade fraud investigation into [MGI International](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-resolves-criminal-trade-fraud-investigation-plastic-resin-distributor), a plastic resin distributor, for falsifying Country of Origin declarations to avoid Section 301 duties — a case that also involved incorrect HTS codes.

**Valuation manipulation.** Double-invoicing schemes — where an importer submits a lower-value invoice to CBP while billing customers at the actual price — are a specific enforcement target.

**Transshipment and tariff evasion.** Routing goods through third countries to avoid country-specific duties is being tracked using Exiger's AI analytics. This includes Section 301, Section 232, and antidumping/countervailing duty evasion.

**Drawback abuse.** Overstating or misrepresenting export volumes to inflate duty drawback claims is monitored as duty drawback programs grow.

## What Should Importers Do Right Now?

The enforcement environment demands specific, concrete actions. These are not optional compliance best practices — they are risk mitigation steps that directly affect your financial exposure.

**1. Standardize HTS classifications across all brokers.** One of the most common problems is different customs brokers classifying the same part under different HTS codes. This creates systematic duty inconsistencies — exactly the kind of data anomaly CBP's analytics tools are designed to flag. GingerControl's HTS Classifier follows GRI logic and asks clarifying questions before assigning a classification — producing audit-ready reports grounded in Section Notes, Chapter Notes, and relevant cross rulings. A single, defensible classification for each product, documented with clear reasoning and references, eliminates this risk.

**2. Audit your country of origin determinations.** If your supply chain involves components manufactured in one country and assembled or finished in another, make sure your origin determinations follow the substantial transformation test and are backed by documentation. If you source from countries subject to Section 301 tariffs, your origin claims will receive extra scrutiny.

**3. Document your valuation methodology.** CBP expects importers to have a clear, consistent, and documented approach to determining the declared value of imported goods. If your methodology is ad hoc or undocumented, you are vulnerable.

**4. Review your duty drawback claims.** If you participate in a duty drawback program, make sure your export documentation, HTS matching, and claim calculations are accurate and traceable.

**5. Consider voluntary self-disclosure.** If you discover compliance problems during an internal review, DOJ has made clear that [voluntary self-disclosure, cooperation, and remediation](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-division-corporate-enforcement) lead to substantially more favorable outcomes. Companies that come forward before being investigated receive significant settlement credit under both DOJ's Corporate Enforcement Policy and the FCA.

**6. Treat compliance as a financial function.** The companies that get caught are typically the ones where compliance sits in a silo, disconnected from finance, procurement, and operations. When trade compliance is integrated into your financial planning, classification errors get caught earlier, duty savings are captured, and audit exposure shrinks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the DOJ Trade Fraud Task Force?

The Trade Fraud Task Force is a cross-agency enforcement initiative [launched in August 2025](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/departments-justice-and-homeland-security-partnering-cross-agency-trade-fraud-task-force) by DOJ in partnership with CBP and Homeland Security Investigations. It is staffed with 40 attorneys and coordinates civil and criminal enforcement against tariff evasion, misclassification, country of origin fraud, and transshipment schemes.

### What penalties can importers face for customs fraud under Section 1592?

Penalties range from 2x unpaid duties for negligence to the full domestic value of the merchandise for fraud under [19 U.S.C. § 1592](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/1592). The False Claims Act adds treble damages — three times the unpaid duties — plus per-claim penalties. Intentional fraud can result in criminal prosecution of individual employees.

### Can whistleblowers report customs fraud?

Yes. Under the False Claims Act's qui tam provisions, anyone with knowledge of customs fraud can [file a case on behalf of the government](https://www.justice.gov/civil/false-claims-act) and receive up to 30% of any recovery. In FY 2025, whistleblowers filed a record 1,297 qui tam lawsuits.

### What is a CF-28 from CBP?

A CF-28 is a Request for Information from CBP asking the importer to provide additional documentation supporting how goods were classified, valued, or declared. Receiving a CF-28 signals that CBP is reviewing your entry data and may change the duty assessment if documentation is insufficient.

### How is CBP using AI to detect trade fraud?

CBP awarded a contract to [Exiger in October 2025](https://www.exiger.com/perspectives/exiger-wins-cbp-contract-detection-of-illicit-transshipment/) for AI-powered detection of illicit transshipment. The platform cross-references import data to identify anomalies in classification, valuation, country of origin, and supply chain routing — flagging patterns like the same product classified under different HTS codes by different brokers.

### How does GingerControl help importers prepare for CBP enforcement?

GingerControl is a pre-classification research tool that follows the same reasoning process a licensed customs broker uses — GRI analysis, Section/Chapter Note review, and cross ruling research. It produces audit-ready documentation that supports classification decisions, helping importers standardize their HTS codes and build defensible records before CBP asks questions. GingerControl does not provide legal advice or replace licensed customs expertise.

### What should I do if I discover compliance errors in my import data?

DOJ has stated that [voluntary self-disclosure, cooperation, and remediation](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-division-corporate-enforcement) lead to substantially more favorable outcomes. Companies that come forward before being investigated receive significant settlement credit. Conduct an internal audit and consult a trade compliance professional before deciding whether to self-disclose.
